Institutional Transaction Cost Analysis

A Python-based study of institutional execution costs, comparing trading strategies through market-impact modeling and Monte Carlo simulation.

Focus
Quantitative Finance | Execution Cost
Data
AAPL intraday market data
01

Business Context

Large institutional orders are usually spread out over time because executing everything at once can move the market. The problem is that waiting has a cost too — the longer an order stays open, the more time there is for the price to move. I used 1-minute intraday data to look at that trade-off.

The question: Which execution strategy offers the best trade-off between transaction cost and execution risk for a large institutional order?

I compared TWAP, VWAP, POV, and IS-Optimal using implementation shortfall, market impact, and execution risk rather than looking at execution cost by itself.

02

Data & Workflow

I started with one-minute AAPL price and volume data and used the intraday patterns to estimate volatility, liquidity, and bid-ask spreads where quote data wasn't available. I used those estimates in the market-impact model, built the execution schedules, and then tested them across simulated price paths.

03

Analysis & Visualizations

I focused the final comparison on the distribution of implementation shortfall, the sources of execution cost, and the trade-off between expected cost and execution risk.

01

Implementation Shortfall

I plotted the full distribution of simulated shortfall because the average only tells part of the story. I wanted to see how consistent each strategy was across different simulated price paths.

02

Execution Costs

I broke execution cost into spread, temporary impact, and permanent impact, with timing risk shown separately. That made it easier to trace where the differences between the strategies were coming from.

03

Cost–Risk Frontier

I put mean implementation shortfall and execution risk on the same chart to bring the two sides of the decision together. It gave me a simple way to judge whether a lower expected cost was coming with more or less risk.

04

Key Findings

1.9 bps IS-Optimal Avg. Cost
01

IS-Optimal delivered the lowest execution cost

Average cost was approximately 1.9 bps, giving IS-Optimal a small advantage over TWAP and VWAP while avoiding the much higher cost associated with POV.

8.3 bps POV Avg. Cost
02

POV's faster execution came at a much higher cost

POV's average cost rose to roughly 8.3 bps, with temporary market impact accounting for most of the increase. Trading more aggressively reduced exposure to price movements, but it was expensive in the model.

3.6 bps Lowest Mean Shortfall
03

The lowest-cost strategy was not the lowest-risk strategy

IS-Optimal recorded the lowest mean implementation shortfall at about 3.6 bps, while POV sat at the lower-risk, higher-cost end of the comparison. Cost alone wasn't enough to decide which strategy performed better.

4 Execution Strategies
04

Execution schedules materially changed the outcome

All four strategies worked the same parent order, but the way they paced the trades led to different levels of market impact, shortfall, and timing exposure. The execution schedule itself made a meaningful difference.

05

Recommendations

I worked with one-minute OHLCV data and estimated spreads, so I'd next check whether the same cost-risk patterns hold with actual quote or order-book data.

I'd add available liquidity, queue depth, and adverse selection to get closer to what happens when an order actually interacts with the market. That would also give me a better way to test the market-impact assumptions in the current model.

The current example is based on AAPL. I'd run the same analysis on securities with different liquidity and volatility profiles, as well as different order sizes, to see where the strategy rankings start to change.

06

Scope & Limitations

I used one-minute AAPL data, so I couldn't see the individual trades or order-book activity behind each price move. I also had to estimate spread and market-impact costs because I didn't have actual institutional orders and fills. The simulation doesn't include queue position, partial fills, or venue selection, so I see the results as a comparison of the four strategies within the model — not as a recreation of live execution.

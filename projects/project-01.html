01

Business Context

I was working with commercial data spread across sales, finance, marketing, inventory, and customer operations, where independently calculated KPIs made cross-functional reporting difficult to reconcile. The challenge was turning data from separate systems into one consistent view of business performance.

The question: How can commercial data across multiple business functions be brought together to create a consistent view of performance, identify areas that need attention, and support better business decisions?

Rather than analyzing each function separately, I brought the data into a governed analytical structure and standardized the reporting measures. This gave me a common view for evaluating revenue, profitability, pipeline, customer performance, inventory risk, and demand.

02

Data & Workflow

I started by staging the synthetic source data so I could catch the issues built into it, including duplicate customer keys, missing emails, and orphaned opportunity records before they reached the warehouse. I then resolved or excluded those records as appropriate, loaded the validated data into a Star Schema with 10 dimensions and 8 fact tables, and logged the validation results so each ETL run had a traceable data-quality record.

03

Analysis & Visualizations

The analysis focuses on customer behavior, revenue exposure, and short-term demand planning.

01

Customer Segmentation

I used RFM segmentation to examine how purchasing frequency, lifetime value, and inactivity differed across the customer base. The goal was to see whether retention and engagement decisions should vary by customer behaviour rather than treating every account the same way.

02

Revenue at Risk

I mapped retention risk against lifetime revenue so customer health could be viewed alongside the commercial value attached to each account. This let me assess prioritization using both risk and financial exposure instead of relying on health or satisfaction scores alone.

03

Demand Forecast

I used a six-month Holt-Winters holdout forecast to test whether historical demand contained enough structure to support short-term planning. I kept the holdout period separate from model fitting so I could evaluate the forecast against demand the model had not already seen.

04

Key Findings

0.38× Pipeline Coverage
01

Pipeline coverage remains thin

Open pipeline covers only 0.38× of the revenue base, leaving relatively little opportunity value available to support future growth. This made pipeline generation and conversion a more immediate concern than the topline trend alone suggested.

$312.5M FY2024 Revenue
02

Revenue growth lost momentum

Revenue increased from $298.8M in FY2022 to $312.5M in FY2024, but the year-over-year trend weakened in 2024. The longer-term increase therefore masks a more recent slowdown in commercial momentum.

SMB Largest Revenue Exposure
03

Revenue exposure is concentrated in SMB

The SMB segment carries the largest share of potential revenue at risk. That concentration gave me a clearer retention priority: the accounts requiring attention are not evenly distributed across the customer base.

1.06% Holdout MAPE
04

Short-term demand was highly forecastable

The Holt-Winters model achieved a 1.06% holdout MAPE, while the ERP forecasting process averaged 87.46% forecast accuracy. The low holdout error gave me a practical reason to bring short-term demand forecasting into inventory planning rather than treating it as a separate analytical exercise.

05

Recommendations

I'd prioritize qualified opportunity generation and examine where conversion is weakening through the active pipeline. That would address future revenue capacity directly rather than relying on recent topline performance as an indicator of what comes next.

I'd focus retention effort first on high-value and critical-risk SMB accounts, using the combination of account value and retention risk to determine where intervention deserves the most attention.

The next step would be to combine short-term demand forecasts with stockout and supplier-performance data when making replenishment decisions, especially for products already showing availability risk.

06

Scope & Limitations

I generated the dataset specifically for this project, so the findings demonstrate the analytical approach rather than the performance of a real company. I also used SQLite to keep the warehouse self-contained; in a production setting, I would move the same structure to a more scalable database environment. Finally, I treated customer retention as a rule-based risk problem because I did not have historical churn outcomes to train against. With that data, I would test a predictive model and validate whether it improves how at-risk accounts are identified.

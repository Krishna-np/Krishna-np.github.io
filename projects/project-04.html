Retail Customer & Marketing Analytics

Retail analytics spanning customer value, retention, campaign engagement, merchandising, cross-sell, store performance, and sales modeling.

Focus
Customer & Marketing Analytics
Data
Dunnhumby Complete Journey
01

Business Context

Customer value, campaign engagement, product relationships, and store performance each showed a different side of the retail business, but looking at them separately would only tell part of the story. I wanted to connect those views and see where the clearest customer and commercial patterns emerged.

The question: How can I use customer, transaction, product, and campaign data together to improve targeting, retention, merchandising, and commercial performance?

I brought customer segmentation, retention, campaign, cross-sell, and store performance analysis together so the findings could be evaluated in the same business context.

02

Data & Workflow

I started with Dunnhumby transaction data covering households, products, campaigns, coupons, and promotions, removing invalid sales and quantity records before joining product information to the purchases. From there, I built customer and commercial measures to compare segments, retention, campaigns, stores, product categories, and basket relationships, then used store-week data to compare Linear Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost sales models.

03

Analysis & Visualizations

I kept the final visuals focused on three parts of the analysis where the choice of view helped me answer a different business question.

01

Customer Value

I used the RFM segment view to see whether customer value was concentrated enough to justify treating customer groups differently. Comparing the segments side by side gave me a practical basis for thinking about retention and re-engagement priorities.

02

Cross-Sell Opportunities

For basket analysis, I compared support, confidence, and lift so I could separate relationships that were simply common from those that showed stronger cross-sell potential. This gave me a more useful way to narrow the associations into candidates for merchandising tests.

03

Store-Week Sales Model

I plotted actual against predicted store-week sales to check how closely the final model tracked observations across the sales range and where prediction errors became more visible. This was a model-evaluation view rather than a forecasting exercise.

04

Key Findings

$7,360.93 Champion Customer Value
01

Customer value is concentrated in the strongest segments

Champions averaged $7,360.93 in customer value, compared with just $896.22 for At Risk households, making the value gap between active and disengaging customers difficult to ignore.

53.3% Week 4 Retention
02

Retention falls quickly after acquisition

By Week 4, median retention across customer cohorts was 53.3%, pointing to the early customer lifecycle as an important window for retention efforts.

$4,491.98 Targeted Household Spend
03

Targeted households were already very different

Targeted households averaged $4,491.98 in spend versus $1,028.54 for non-targeted households, showing a substantial difference in purchasing behaviour between the two groups.

Meat–Seafood Cross-Sell Relationship
04

Basket relationships surfaced practical cross-sell opportunities

Recurring category relationships included Meat–Seafood (Packaged) and Deli–Pastry, providing specific combinations that could be tested through merchandising or targeted offers.

05

Recommendations

Based on the value and retention patterns, I'd protect Champions and Loyal customers while testing earlier re-engagement for At Risk households.

I'd use treatment and holdout groups to determine whether campaign activity changes customer spending or engagement rather than relying on differences between targeted and non-targeted households.

I'd take the strongest basket associations into product-placement, bundle, or targeted-offer tests and measure whether they change basket behaviour.

06

Scope & Limitations

I treated the campaign, coupon, display, and mailer results as observational rather than causal, since the data does not show the incremental impact of each marketing activity. Customer measures are limited to the available 102 weeks, while the store analysis lacks geography, format, operating costs, and margin data that would allow a deeper profitability view. I also treated the store-week model as a performance model rather than a forecast because it uses same-period features. With additional data, I would use controlled campaign testing, richer store economics, and lagged features for forward-looking sales forecasting.
